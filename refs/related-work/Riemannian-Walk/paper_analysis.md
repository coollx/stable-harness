Classified as a **method paper**: the dominant contribution is a built, reproducible training procedure — RWalk, a quadratic parameter-importance penalty combining an online Fisher matrix with a path-accumulated importance score, plus a small replay subset — which is what the title names and what every results table benchmarks. The paper's other contribution, two evaluation measures and a protocol, is a definitional contribution; it is folded into section 2a (as the evaluation half of the pipeline), given the paper's own notation in full in section 2c, compared formula-against-formula with neighbouring measures in section 3a, and treated at length in section 5d, because the measures — not the regularizer — are what this reference is here for.

**Why this reference matters here**: `docs/framing.md` claims that greedy per-batch harness patching trades away three future quantities — retention of earlier tasks, generalization to later tasks, and the ability to keep patching — and the third has no standard name or formula in our vocabulary. This paper supplies both: it names that quantity *intransigence*, gives it a formula against an explicit reference model, and shows in weight space the exact trade-off our claim asserts, namely that turning up a retention mechanism buys lower forgetting by paying in intransigence (Table 3). Section 5d states how each measure would be computed for a harness under the score-before-update protocol and which reference model replaces the paper's.

Short names used below: RWalk (the paper's method), EWC (elastic weight consolidation, Kirkpatrick et al. 2016, a baseline), EWC++ (the paper's cheaper online version of EWC), PI (path integral / synaptic intelligence, Zenke et al. 2017, a baseline), iCaRL (Rebuffi et al. 2017, a baseline), GEM (gradient episodic memory, Lopez-Paz and Ranzato 2017, analysed in `refs/related-work/GEM/`), and mean-of-features (the iCaRL sample-selection rule, which the paper abbreviates MoF).

## 1. Method Motivation

### a) Why was this method proposed?

Two reasons, of which the second is the paper's opening move. First, the incremental-learning literature of 2016–2018 "lacks a precise problem definition, proper evaluation settings, and metrics tailored specifically for the IL problem" (Abstract), so published numbers were not comparable. Second, a model that learns tasks one after another must both *preserve* and *update* its knowledge, and the authors argue these are two separate failures that need two separate numbers: "(1) *forgetting*: catastrophically forgetting knowledge of previous tasks; and (2) *intransigence*: inability to update the knowledge to learn a new task. Both of these problems require contradicting solutions and pose a trade-off for any IL algorithm" (Section 1). RWalk is then proposed as the method that sits at a better point on that trade-off than existing regularizers.

### b) Pain points of existing methods

- **Evaluation hides the problem.** Multi-head evaluation — where the model is told at test time which task it is facing, and predicts only within that task's labels — makes every regularized method look solved. The paper measures this: on incremental MNIST under multi-head evaluation, EWC, PI and RWalk all reach 99.3% average accuracy with forgetting at or below 0.003 and intransigence at 0.01 (Table 1), which "gives an impression that IL problem is solved" (Section 6.1, the authors' italics). The same methods under single-head evaluation reach 55.8%, 57.6% and 82.5%.
- **Average accuracy alone is uninformative.** "The higher the $A_k$ the better the classifier, but this does not provide any information about forgetting or intransigence profile of the IL algorithm which would be crucial to judge its behaviour" (Section 3).
- **The existing retention measure is blind to the learning process.** GEM's backward transfer compares a task's final accuracy to its accuracy immediately after it was learned. The paper argues this "makes the measure agnostic to the IL process and does not effectively capture forgetting" — it misses the case where a task's accuracy *rose* after it was learned and then fell back (Section 3, the worked example reproduced in section 2c below).
- **There is no measure of the second failure at all.** Nothing in the prior literature quantifies inability to learn the new task; the authors credit the naming of the concept to a colleague in the Acknowledgements.
- **EWC is expensive and rigid.** EWC stores one Fisher matrix per task, so memory grows as $\mathcal{O}(kP)$ for $k$ tasks and $P$ parameters, "practically infeasible if there are many tasks and the network has millions of parameters", and it needs an extra full forward-backward pass over each task's dataset at the end of that task (Section 4.1). PI and EWC are also "highly sensitive to $\lambda$", the regularization strength (Section 4.1).

### c) Core hypothesis / intuition

Regularizing a network toward its old parameters trades one failure for another along a single dial, so an incremental learner should be judged by two numbers rather than one; and a parameter-importance score that is accumulated along the whole optimization path, measured as loss change per unit of distance travelled in the geometry induced by the Fisher matrix, and then averaged across tasks so the penalty does not grow without bound, sits at a better point on that trade-off than importance measured only at each task's minimum.

## 2. Method Design (PRIMARY FOCUS)

The paper has two pipelines: an evaluation pipeline (what is measured and how) and a training pipeline (RWalk). They are independent — the measures apply to any method, and RWalk can be evaluated by any measures.

### a) Pipeline overview: Input to Processing to Output

**The evaluation pipeline.**

*Input.* A stream of tasks. Task $k$ has dataset $\mathcal{D}_k = \{(\mathbf{x}^k_i, y^k_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_k}$ with a label set $\mathbf{y}^k$ specific to that task, and the label sets are disjoint (Section 2.1). This is the class-incremental construction: incremental MNIST is five tasks of two consecutive digits, $\{\{0,1\}, \{2,3\}, \dots, \{8,9\}\}$; incremental CIFAR is CIFAR-100 split into ten tasks of ten classes, $\{\{0\text{–}9\}, \dots, \{90\text{–}99\}\}$ (Section 6).

*Step 1 — choose the head setting.* At test time the task identifier may or may not be given. **Multi-head**: the identifier $k$ is given, the prediction is made over $\mathcal{Y}^k = \mathbf{y}^k$, the current task's labels only. **Single-head**: the identifier is unknown, the prediction is made over $\mathcal{Y}^k = \cup_{j=1}^{k} \mathbf{y}^j$, every label seen so far (Section 2.1). The paper's worked example: on MNIST with five tasks, at the fifth task multi-head predicts one of $\{8, 9\}$ while single-head predicts one of $\{0, \dots, 9\}$. The paper argues single-head is the setting that matters — it is harder because the classifier must also learn inter-task discrimination with no or limited access to previous tasks' data, and multi-head "requires extra supervisory signals, in the form of task descriptors, at test time", to the point that "if the task contains only one label, multi-head evaluation would be equivalent to knowing the ground truth label itself" (Section 2.1). Both settings are reported throughout, which is what makes the paper's central empirical point visible.

*Step 2 — fill the accuracy table.* Let $a_{k,j} \in [0,1]$ be the accuracy on the held-out test set of task $j$ after training incrementally on tasks 1 to $k$, with $j \le k$ (Section 3). This is a lower-triangular table: the paper never evaluates a task before it has been trained on, so there is no entry for $j > k$.

*Step 3 — train the reference model for intransigence.* Separately from the stream, train a standard (non-incremental) classifier on the union of all data up to task $k$, $\bigcup_{l=1}^{k}\mathcal{D}_l$, and record its accuracy on task $k$'s held-out set as $a_k^*$ (Section 3). This is an extra model, not an entry of the table.

*Step 4 — read off three numbers.* Average accuracy $A_k$, forgetting $F_k$, intransigence $I_k$ (the formulas are in section 2c). The paper reports them at the final task of each stream: $A_5, F_5, I_5$ for MNIST and $A_{10}, F_{10}, I_{10}$ for CIFAR-100, in both head settings, alongside the regularization strength $\lambda$ used (Table 1). It also plots per-task accuracy against stream position for every task separately (Figs. 2 and 6) and the pair $(I_k, F_k)$ as a point in a quadrant plot whose four quadrants are the sign combinations of backward and forward transfer (Fig. 3).

**The training pipeline (RWalk).** Given parameters $\theta^{k-1}$ trained on tasks 1 to $k-1$ and the new dataset $\mathcal{D}_k$:

1. **Maintain an online Fisher matrix.** Instead of EWC's one Fisher per task, keep a single diagonal Fisher updated by a moving average during training (eq. 6), so memory is $\mathcal{O}(P)$ regardless of how many tasks have been seen, and no extra pass over the task's data is needed at the boundary. Only two Fisher matrices exist at any moment: the running one and the one frozen at the end of the previous task. The authors call this EWC++ and note that Schwarz et al. (2018) developed the same thing concurrently as online EWC.
2. **Accumulate a path-based importance score.** Every $\Delta t$ training iterations, add to each parameter's score the loss decrease it produced divided by the distance it travelled, where distance is measured by the Fisher rather than by Euclidean length (eq. 7 and the score expression below it). Negative contributions are clipped to zero. Using Euclidean distance instead recovers PI.
3. **Penalize movement, weighted by both.** Train on $\mathcal{D}_k$ with the ordinary loss plus $\lambda$ times a quadratic penalty on the distance from $\theta^{k-1}$, weighted by the sum of the frozen Fisher and the accumulated score (eq. 8). Both weights are individually normalized to $[0,1]$ so the two terms have comparable scale.
4. **Average the scores at each task boundary** so the penalty does not become monotonically more rigid as tasks accumulate: the new score is the mean of the old accumulated score and the score accumulated over the last task. The paper states this has "a positive side-effect of the regularization hyperparameter $\lambda$ being less sensitive to the number of tasks" (Section 4.1), which Table 3 then verifies.
5. **Keep a small replay subset for intransigence.** Store $m$ points per task (at most 5% of the data) and mix them into training on the current task. This is the only component aimed at intransigence; the first two are aimed at forgetting (Section 4).

*Output.* The trained network plus the lower-triangular accuracy table; the three measures are computed from the table and the reference model.

### b) Architecture components

No new architecture. MNIST: a fully-connected network with two hidden layers of 256 rectified linear units. CIFAR-100: four convolutional layers (two of 32 filters, max-pool with dropout 0.5, two of 64 filters, max-pool with dropout 0.5), all kernels $3\times3$ with stride 1, followed by a *single* fully-connected layer whose weights are "dynamically added" per task — one block of $n$ outputs appended for each new task (supplementary Table 4). In the single-head setting all appended blocks are active at test time; in multi-head only the current task's block is. Adam with learning rate $1\times10^{-3}$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, batch size 64 in every experiment (Section 6). Method-specific state is one running Fisher, one frozen Fisher, one accumulated score vector, one copy of $\theta^{k-1}$ — all $\mathcal{O}(P)$, "independent of the number of tasks" (Section 4.1) — plus the stored samples.

### c) Formulas and algorithms

**The three measures (Section 3).** These are the part of the paper this reference exists for, so they are given in full.

*Average accuracy.* With $a_{k,j}$ the accuracy on task $j$'s held-out set after incremental training up to task $k$,
$$A_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k} a_{k,j}.$$
Higher is better. The output space over which $a_{k,j}$ is computed is either $\mathbf{y}^j$ or $\cup_{j=1}^{k}\mathbf{y}^j$ depending on the head setting, so every number below is setting-specific.

*Forgetting measure (eq. 3).* For task $j$ after the model has been trained up to task $k > j$,
$$f_j^k = \max_{l \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}} a_{l,j} - a_{k,j}, \qquad \forall j < k,$$
with $f_j^k \in [-1, 1]$, and the average over previous tasks
$$F_k = \frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} f_j^k .$$
In plain words: for each earlier task, take the best score it ever reached at any point in the stream, subtract the score it has now, and average over earlier tasks. Lower is better. The reference point is the *maximum over the whole learning process*, not the score at the moment the task was learned — that is the deliberate difference from GEM's backward transfer, and it makes $F_k$ at least as large as the accuracy drop measured from the diagonal. The paper's own worked example (Section 3, with the subscripts written in the reverse order to eq. 3's convention — the intended reading is the accuracy on task 1 after training up to tasks 1, 2, 3, 4): accuracies $\{0.7, 0.8, 0.6, 0.5\}$ give $f_1^4 = 0.8 - 0.5 = 0.3$ under eq. 3, whereas GEM's definition would report $0.7 - 0.5 = 0.2$ "(irrespective of the variations in $a_{1,2}$ and $a_{1,3}$). Hence, it does not capture the fact that there was a PBT in the learning process." A negative $f_j^k$ is positive backward transfer, meaning a later task improved an earlier one; a positive $f_j^k$ is negative backward transfer.

*Intransigence measure (eq. 4).*
$$I_k = a_k^* - a_{k,k},$$
with $I_k \in [-1, 1]$ and lower better. In plain words: train an ordinary, non-incremental classifier on all the data seen so far and score it on the new task; subtract the incremental learner's own score on the new task, measured right after it finished training on it; the gap is how much of the new task the incremental learner failed to learn.

**What the reference model is, exactly, and what that choice implies.** The paper is explicit: "Since we wish to quantify the *inability* to learn, we compare to the standard classification model which has access to all the datasets at all times. We train a reference/target model with dataset $\bigcup_{l=1}^{k}\mathcal{D}_l$ and measure its accuracy on the held-out set of the $k$-th task, denoted as $a_k^*$" (Section 3). So the reference is **a model trained jointly on all data up to and including the new task — not a model trained on the new task alone, and not an untrained model**. Four consequences follow.

1. *It is an offline joint-training ceiling, not a specialist ceiling.* The reference is the best a conventional learner can do on task $k$ when nothing is withheld. Because it has also seen every earlier task, in the single-head setting it has no excuse for confusing task $k$'s classes with earlier ones — so $I_k$ charges the incremental learner both for underfitting the new task and for failing to discriminate it from old ones. The paper says as much: "The effect of intransigence is more prominent in the single-head setting especially in the absence of previous data, as the model is expected to learn to differentiate the current task from the previous ones" (Section 3).
2. *It is expensive and is meant to be approximated.* One extra joint training run per value of $k$. The paper reports only the final $k$, so one reference model per dataset and head setting, and it explicitly licenses substitution: "A reasonable reference/target model can be defined depending on the feasibility to obtain it. In situations where it is highly expensive, an approximation can be proposed" (Section 3).
3. *It is not computable from the accuracy table.* Unlike $A_k$ and $F_k$, intransigence needs a model that was never part of the stream. Anyone reporting it must say which reference they used.
4. *Negative intransigence is not automatically good news.* The paper reads $I_k < 0$ as positive forward transfer and $I_k > 0$ as negative forward transfer. But in the single-head setting an incremental model that has collapsed onto the newest classes can beat the joint reference on the newest task precisely because it has stopped predicting the old ones. Table 1 shows this: on CIFAR-100 single-head, the unregularized baseline has $I_{10} = -0.06$ while its average accuracy is 10.2%, and the same baseline with stored samples has $I_{10} = -0.3$ with average accuracy 12.9% and forgetting 0.64. Intransigence read alone is misleading; the paper's own answer is Fig. 3, which always plots it against forgetting.

*Relation to the transfer vocabulary.* The paper maps its two measures onto the transfer language of GEM: negative forgetting means positive backward transfer, positive intransigence means negative forward transfer, and the ideal model sits in the quadrant where both are negative (Section 3 and Fig. 3).

**RWalk's equations (Section 4).**

*KL-divergence as the distance (eqs. 1–2).* The empirical Fisher information matrix at $\theta$ is $F_\theta = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(\frac{\partial \log p_\theta(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\theta}\right)\left(\frac{\partial \log p_\theta(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\theta}\right)^{\!\top}\right]$ (eq. 1), and for a small step the KL divergence between the network's old and new conditional likelihoods is $D_{KL}(p_\theta \| p_{\theta+\Delta\theta}) \approx \frac{1}{2}\Delta\theta^\top F_\theta \Delta\theta$, which with a diagonal approximation becomes $\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{P} F_{\theta_i}\Delta\theta_i^2$ (eq. 2). Appendix A proves the approximation and explains why the empirical Fisher, not the true Fisher, is used (the true Fisher needs multiple backward passes per example).

*EWC++ objective (eq. 5).* $\arg\min_\theta \tilde{L}^k(\theta) := L^k(\theta) + \lambda\, D_{KL}\!\left(p_{\theta^{k-1}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\right)$, which under eq. 2 is $L^k(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_{\theta_i^{k-1}}(\theta_i - \theta_i^{k-1})^2$ — "for two tasks, the above regularization is exactly the same as that of EWC" (Section 4.1), re-derived from a KL-divergence rather than a Bayesian argument.

*Online Fisher (eq. 6).* $F^t_\theta = \alpha F^t_\theta + (1-\alpha)F^{t-1}_\theta$ with $\alpha \in [0,1]$ and $F^t_\theta$ computed from the current batch; $t$ indexes training iterations, not tasks. At each task boundary the running value is frozen as $F_{\theta^{k-1}}$ and used to regularize the next task.

*Path-based importance (eq. 7 and the expression following it).* By a first-order Taylor expansion the loss change decomposes per parameter, $L(\theta(t+\Delta t)) - L(\theta(t)) \approx -\sum_{i=1}^{P}\Delta L_t^{t+\Delta t}(\theta_i)$, and the importance of parameter $i$ accumulated from iteration $t_1$ to $t_2$ is
$$s_{t_1}^{t_2}(\theta_i) = \sum_{t=t_1}^{t_2} \frac{\Delta L_t^{t+\Delta t}(\theta_i)}{\frac{1}{2}F^t_{\theta_i}\Delta\theta_i(t)^2 + \epsilon}, \qquad \Delta\theta_i(t) = \theta_i(t+\Delta t) - \theta_i(t),\ \epsilon > 0 .$$
In plain words: a parameter is important in proportion to how much loss it removed per unit of change it caused in the network's output distribution. "Since we care about the positive influence of the parameters, negative scores are set to zero." Replacing the Fisher-weighted denominator by squared Euclidean distance recovers PI.

*Final objective (eq. 8).*
$$\tilde{L}^k(\theta) = L^k(\theta) + \lambda \sum_{i=1}^{P}\left(F_{\theta_i^{k-1}} + s_{t_0}^{t_{k-1}}(\theta_i)\right)\left(\theta_i - \theta_i^{k-1}\right)^2 ,$$
with the boundary averaging $s_{t_0}^{t_{k-1}}(\theta_i) = \frac{1}{2}\left(s_{t_0}^{t_{k-2}}(\theta_i) + s_{t_{k-2}}^{t_{k-1}}(\theta_i)\right)$ and both weight terms individually normalized to $[0,1]$.

*Sample selection (Section 4.2).* Four rules for choosing the $m$ stored points per task: uniform random; plane-distance, which favours points near the decision boundary using the pseudo-distance $d(\mathbf{x}_i) = \phi(\mathbf{x}_i)^\top w^{y_i}$ and sampling probability $q(\mathbf{x}_i)\propto 1/d(\mathbf{x}_i)$; entropy of the softmax output, favouring uncertain points; and mean-of-features, iCaRL's rule, which greedily picks points whose running feature mean tracks the class feature mean, at cost $\mathcal{O}(nfm)$ for $n$ examples, $f$ feature dimensions and $m$ selected points.

## 3. Comparison with Existing Methods

### a) Fundamental differences

*Against other regularizers.* EWC and PI both penalize movement away from old parameters weighted by an importance score; they differ in where the score comes from. EWC uses the Fisher at each task's local minimum, which "is nearly zero whenever $\tilde{L}^k \approx L^k$", giving "negligible regularization leading to catastrophic forgetting" (Section 4.1). PI uses loss change per unit of Euclidean parameter movement along the path. RWalk uses both: a Fisher kept online through training rather than only at the minimum, plus a path score whose denominator is Fisher-weighted distance, so the score measures loss reduction per unit of change in the network's *output distribution* rather than in its parameters. Two structural differences follow: memory is $\mathcal{O}(P)$ rather than EWC's $\mathcal{O}(kP)$, and the score is averaged rather than summed at task boundaries, so the penalty does not become monotonically more rigid as the stream lengthens.

*Against replay and distillation.* iCaRL regularizes activations and classifies by nearest exemplar mean; the paper's own runs find it "significantly worse than what has been reported by the authors" and attribute this to the smaller, less expressive convolutional network used here in place of a residual network (Section 6.1). RWalk stores samples too, but uses them only as extra training data alongside the parameter penalty.

*Against GEM.* GEM constrains gradients so no past task's memory loss increases. RWalk instead penalizes parameter movement. The comparison is only in the supplementary, on residual networks under the multi-head setting (Table 2): iCaRL 50.8%, GEM 65.4%, RWalk 70.1% average accuracy on CIFAR-100 split into 20 tasks of 5 classes, with 5120 stored samples for the first two and 5000 for RWalk. The authors note "comparison with GEM is not available in the main paper" (Supplementary preamble).

**Forgetting versus backward transfer, formula against formula.** GEM defines backward transfer over $T$ tasks from its accuracy matrix $R$, where $R_{i,j}$ is the accuracy on task $j$ after finishing task $i$ — the same object as $a_{i,j}$ here:
$$\mathrm{BWT} = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}\left(R_{T,i} - R_{i,i}\right) = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{j<T}\left(a_{T,j} - a_{j,j}\right),$$
against this paper's
$$F_T = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{j<T}\left(\max_{l<T} a_{l,j} - a_{T,j}\right).$$
The two differ in sign convention (higher backward transfer is better, lower forgetting is better) and in the reference point: GEM anchors each task at its own diagonal entry $a_{j,j}$, this paper anchors it at the maximum ever reached. Therefore $F_T \ge -\mathrm{BWT}$ always, with equality exactly when every task peaked at the moment it was learned. The two numbers are not interchangeable and the paper's is the stricter one. The paper's justification is that the maximum records positive backward transfer that happened and was then lost, which the diagonal anchor cannot see (the 0.3 versus 0.2 example in section 2c).

**Intransigence versus forward transfer.** GEM defines forward transfer as
$$\mathrm{FWT} = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{i=2}^{T}\left(R_{i-1,i} - \bar b_i\right),$$
the accuracy on task $i$ *before any of its data has been seen*, minus the accuracy of a randomly initialized network. This paper's intransigence is $I_k = a_k^* - a_{k,k}$: the accuracy on task $k$ *after* training on it, subtracted from a jointly trained model's accuracy. They read different cells and use different references. GEM's forward transfer reads the cell one step above the diagonal against a floor (an untrained network); intransigence reads the diagonal itself against a ceiling (a model with full data access). Neither is derivable from the other: GEM's accuracy matrix contains no $a_k^*$, and this paper's lower-triangular table contains no $R_{i-1,i}$. The paper states the relationship itself: "in [GEM], FT is quantified as the gain in accuracy compared to the random guess (not a measure of intransigence) which is complementary to our approach" (Section 3). The one place they touch is the sign language: $I_k < 0$ is called positive forward transfer here, which is a different event from a large positive $\mathrm{FWT}$.

**Intransigence versus loss of plasticity.** `refs/related-work/Loss-of-Plasticity/paper_analysis.md` records that the Nature 2024 paper never gives plasticity a formula and operationalizes it as *performance attainable on the task currently being learned, benchmarked against a learner that did not go through the stream* — a linear network, a fully reinitialized network, or, in its class-incremental CIFAR-100 experiment, "a network retrained from scratch on exactly the classes available at that point". Intransigence is that quantity written down: same subtraction, same position in the stream, different reference model (jointly trained rather than retrained from scratch — on the class-incremental protocol these are nearly the same model). The differences that matter are three. First, **scope**: intransigence is a scalar at one task, loss of plasticity is the *trend* of that scalar against stream position; this paper reports $I_k$ only at the final task and never plots it against $k$, so it defines the measure without measuring the decline. The Nature paper does plot the trend, and its class-incremental curve — starting at about +2% above the retrained network and reaching about −5% at 100 classes — is, up to sign, intransigence moving from about $-0.02$ to about $+0.05$. Second, **contamination**: in the single-head setting intransigence also charges the learner for confusing the new task's classes with old ones, which is an interference failure rather than a failure to optimize; the Nature paper's protocols avoid this. Third, **neither is our $\Phi(h)$**: `docs/framing.md` defines harness plasticity as the discounted future performance of the whole stream, and both of these measures look one task ahead with no discounting, so they are the one-step special case, as already noted in the Loss-of-Plasticity analysis. The accurate statement is that intransigence is one measurement of loss of plasticity, not a different quantity and not a superset.

### b) Key innovations and their significance

1. **The intransigence measure and its reference model** — *significant*. It gives the second failure of incremental learning a name, a formula, and an explicit comparison model, and it is the first measure in this literature that requires training a model outside the stream. Every later paper that reports "accuracy relative to a jointly trained or retrained model" is using this shape.
2. **The forgetting measure with a maximum-over-the-process reference** — *notable*. A one-symbol change from backward transfer that makes the measure sensitive to gains that happened and were lost. It is also what makes the measure harder to compare across papers, since the two conventions coexist in the literature without always being distinguished.
3. **Insisting on reporting both head settings** — *notable, and the paper's most durable empirical point*. Not a technique, but Table 1 and Section 6.1 make an evaluation choice that had been treated as incidental into a result: the same methods look solved under one and broken under the other.
4. **EWC++: online Fisher by moving average** — *notable*, and honestly presented as concurrent with Schwarz et al.'s online EWC. It removes EWC's per-task storage and its extra pass over the data.
5. **Fisher-weighted path importance plus boundary averaging** — *incremental to notable*. The change from PI is replacing Euclidean distance with distance in the geometry induced by the Fisher; the averaging is what keeps the penalty from stiffening. Together they buy insensitivity to $\lambda$ (Table 3), which is a real practical gain, more clearly demonstrated than the accuracy gain.
6. **The four sample-selection rules** — *incremental*. Fig. 5 shows uniform random sampling "is as good as more complex MoF, Plane Distance (PD) and entropy-based sampling strategies" for the regularized methods (Section 6.1), which undercuts three of the four.

### c) Applicability

RWalk fits streams with many tasks and a tight memory budget, where EWC's per-task Fisher storage is prohibitive and where a joint retrain is out of reach — exactly the regime where the $\mathcal{O}(P)$ state and the $\lambda$-insensitivity pay. It fits poorly where a large replay budget is available: Fig. 4 shows the unregularized baseline catching up once enough samples are stored, needing only 20 samples (0.4% of the data) on MNIST, though 200 (40% of the previous tasks' samples) on CIFAR-100. The measures themselves apply to any learner scored per task; the paper notes that "some other task specific measure of correctness (e.g., IoU for object segmentation) can also be used while the definitions of forgetting and intransigence remain the same" (Section 3), and the Discussion names segmentation as the target application.

### d) Comparison table

## 4. Experimental Validation

### a) Experimental design

Two streams. **Incremental MNIST**: five tasks of two consecutive digits, fully-connected network with two hidden layers of 256 rectified linear units. **Incremental CIFAR**: CIFAR-100 in ten tasks of ten classes, the four-convolution network of supplementary Table 4 with one per-task block appended to the single dense layer. Adam, learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 64 throughout. Baselines: Vanilla (no regularization), EWC (meaning EWC++, since "we observed that EWC++ performed at least as good as EWC and therefore, in all the experiments, by EWC we mean the stronger baseline EWC++"), PI, iCaRL and iCaRL-hb1 (the hybrid version using the standard neural classifier). Both head settings are reported. In the single-head setting the baselines are additionally run with stored samples, marked with a trailing "-S", to make the comparison to RWalk fair — RWalk always uses samples. Stored-sample budgets: 10 samples per previous class on MNIST (0.2% of the data) and 25 on CIFAR-100 (5%), selected by mean-of-features (Table 1 caption). Supplementary settings: $\alpha = 0.9$ everywhere, $\Delta t = 10$ for MNIST and 50 for CIFAR-100 (B.3). Supplementary B.1 adds a residual-network comparison to GEM and iCaRL on CIFAR-100 split into 20 tasks of 5 classes, multi-head, following GEM's own setup. The number of random seeds per configuration is not stated anywhere in the paper; error bars appear in Figs. 2 and 4 without a stated run count. [Information not available in provided text]

### b) Key results

1. **Multi-head evaluation makes every regularized method look finished; single-head evaluation does not.** MNIST multi-head: Vanilla 90.3% accuracy, forgetting 0.12, intransigence $6.6\times10^{-4}$; EWC, PI and RWalk all 99.3% with forgetting 0.001–0.003 and intransigence 0.01. MNIST single-head: Vanilla 38.0% / 0.62 / 0.29; EWC 55.8% / 0.08 / 0.77; PI 57.6% / 0.11 / 0.80; iCaRL 55.8% / 0.19 / 0.46; RWalk (with samples) 82.5% / 0.15 / 0.14. CIFAR-100 multi-head: Vanilla 44.4%, EWC 72.8%, PI 73.2%, RWalk 74.2%. CIFAR-100 single-head: Vanilla 10.2%, EWC 23.1%, PI 22.8%, and with samples Vanilla-S 12.9%, EWC-S 33.6%, PI-S 33.6%, RWalk 34.0% (Table 1). The paper's summary: "Although regularized methods, EWC and PI, designed to counter catastrophic forgetting, result in less degradation of forgetting, their accuracy is still significantly worse — compare 99.3% of PI in multi-head against 57.6% in single-head" (Section 6.1).
2. **Forgetting and intransigence move in opposite directions as regularization strength rises, and this is the paper's clearest evidence for its central claim.** Table 3, MNIST: EWC at $\lambda = 75$, $75\times10^3$, $75\times10^5$ gives forgetting 0.19, 0.15, 0.13 and intransigence 0.10, 0.21, 0.24 — a change of $-0.06$ in forgetting bought with $+0.14$ in intransigence over a $10^5$ range of $\lambda$; PI over the same range gives $-0.07$ and $+0.13$. RWalk over the same range changes by 0 in both. On CIFAR-100 the same pattern holds for EWC ($-0.11$ forgetting, $+0.14$ intransigence) and is flat for RWalk.
3. **A handful of stored samples removes most of the intransigence.** "In the case of PI on MNIST with only 10 ($\approx 0.2\%$) samples for each previous class, the intransigence drops from 0.8 to 0.05 which results in improving the average accuracy from 57.6% to 78.7%" (Section 6.1). The same comparison for EWC is 0.77 to 0.22 with accuracy 55.8% to 79.7%; for Vanilla 0.29 to 0.03 with accuracy 38.0% to 73.7% (Table 1). On CIFAR-100 with 5% samples, "almost identical behaviour is observed".
4. **RWalk has the best average accuracy in every row of Table 1 and in the residual-network comparison.** The margins over the next-best method are 1.0 point on CIFAR-100 multi-head (74.2 versus 73.2), 3.8 points on MNIST single-head over the best sample-using baseline (82.5 versus 78.7), and 0.4 points on CIFAR-100 single-head (34.0 versus 33.6). In the supplementary residual-network setting RWalk reaches 70.1% against GEM's 65.4% and iCaRL's 50.8% (Table 2).
5. **Uniform random sample selection is as good as the elaborate rules, for regularized methods.** Fig. 5: mean-of-features is best overall, but "*uniform* sampling, despite being simple, is as good as more complex MoF, Plane Distance (PD) and entropy-based sampling strategies. Furthermore, the regularized methods remain insensitive to different sampling strategies, whereas in Vanilla, performance varies a lot" (Section 6.1).

### c) Where it shines

The single-head MNIST column is where the method's margin is real: 82.5% against 79.7% for the strongest sample-using baseline, with forgetting 0.15 and intransigence 0.14, and Fig. 3 places RWalk closest to the origin of the forgetting-versus-intransigence plane — "for models with comparable accuracy, a model which has the smallest distance from (0,0) would be better. As evident, RWalk is closest to (0,0)" (Section 6.1). The insensitivity result is the other place it shines and is cleaner than the accuracy result, because it is a controlled sweep rather than a single tuned point: across five orders of magnitude of $\lambda$ on both datasets, RWalk's forgetting and intransigence do not move (Table 3).

### d) Limitations

*Acknowledged.* The reference model for intransigence may be too expensive and may need approximation (Section 3). iCaRL's underperformance relative to its own published numbers is attributed to the smaller network rather than resolved (Section 6.1). On CIFAR-100 the methods land in different quadrants of Fig. 3, so "their comparison of forgetting and intransigence becomes application specific".

*Implicit.*
- **No stated run count and no significance testing.** Error bars appear in two figures with no sample size given, and the headline table has none at all. The CIFAR-100 single-head margin over EWC-S and PI-S is 0.4 points and the multi-head margin over PI is 1.0 point; neither is defensible from the evidence shown.
- **The measures are reported only at the final task.** $F_k$ and $I_k$ are defined for every $k$ but published only for $k = 5$ and $k = 10$. The trend of intransigence against stream position — the thing that would demonstrate a progressive loss of the ability to learn — is never plotted, although the per-task figures (Figs. 2 and 6) contain the material for the forgetting half.
- **Short streams.** Five and ten tasks in the main paper, twenty in the supplementary. The paper argues that averaging the importance scores keeps the penalty from stiffening as tasks accumulate, but the longest stream shown is 20 tasks and the argument is never tested at length.
- **Intransigence in the single-head setting conflates two failures.** Inability to fit the new task and inability to separate it from old classes both raise $I_k$, and the paper's own explanation of why single-head intransigence is large is the second, not the first (Section 4.2: "at test time the label space is over all the tasks seen so far ... This in turn increases *confusion* at test time").
- **Negative intransigence is reported as positive forward transfer without qualification**, including for a model at 10.2% average accuracy on a 100-class problem (Table 1, CIFAR-100 single-head Vanilla, $I_{10} = -0.06$; Vanilla-S, $I_{10} = -0.3$ at 12.9% accuracy with forgetting 0.64).
- **Four constants, one ablation.** $\lambda$ is swept; $\alpha = 0.9$, $\Delta t \in \{10, 50\}$, the clipping of negative scores, the score-averaging weight of one half, and the normalization to $[0,1]$ are all fixed without ablation, and the paper attributes its best property ($\lambda$-insensitivity) to two of the unablated ones.
- **Every task is image classification with the same loss.** The stream is non-stationary in content but homogeneous in kind, and nothing recurs.

## 5. Reproduction and Application

### a) Open source?

There is no dedicated code release for this paper. The project page hosts the paper, supplementary, poster and slides only, and neither the arXiv, CVF nor Springer pages link to a repository. The closest authorial code is the first author's later repository for Averaged Gradient Episodic Memory, https://github.com/facebookresearch/agem (MIT licence), which lists this paper in its citation block, implements RWalk as one of its selectable methods, and implements the forgetting measure exactly as eq. 3. It is cloned to `repo/` and analysed in `repo_analysis.md`. Three caveats stated there and repeated here: that repository reproduces the ICLR 2019 A-GEM tables, not this paper's, so its task splits and hyperparameters differ; the intransigence measure is not implemented anywhere in it; and neither is the single-head evaluation setting, since every logit mask in the code is set to one task's own labels rather than to the union of labels seen so far. To reproduce this paper's tables one would still have to write the evaluation harness — the joint reference model in particular — from the paper's description. Widely used third-party reimplementations of RWalk exist in Avalanche, FACIL and PyCIL; none is authorial and none is cloned here.

### b) Implementation details requiring attention

From the paper: $\alpha = 0.9$ and $\Delta t = 10$ (MNIST) or 50 (CIFAR-100); the two weight terms in eq. 8 must be normalized to $[0,1]$ individually or the Fisher and path terms will not be commensurate; negative path scores are clipped to zero; scores are averaged, not summed, at each task boundary; the CIFAR-100 network uses one dense layer with a per-task block appended, not a separate head stack. From the code (details and line numbers in `repo_analysis.md`): the Fisher moving average is applied every $\Delta t$ iterations rather than every step; the path-score denominator drops the factor of one half and uses a constant of $10^{-3}$ for $\epsilon$; and the released score-averaging step is an accumulation, `score += (score + omega) / 2`, where the paper's formula is the assignment `score = (score + omega) / 2` — the released code's scores therefore grow across tasks where the paper's are averaged. Anyone reimplementing should follow the paper here, not the repository.

### c) Transferability

The two measures need only per-task scores and, for intransigence, one model trained outside the stream; they carry to any learner and any correctness measure, which the paper states explicitly. The RWalk regularizer needs gradients, a Fisher matrix and a parameter vector, so it transfers to any differentiable learner trained on a task sequence and to nothing else. The evaluation protocol's single-head versus multi-head distinction transfers to any setting where a system may or may not be told which kind of input it is facing.

### d) Transfer to our own work

**Where this paper sits against our claim.** `docs/framing.md` names three future quantities that current-batch scoring cannot see: retention of earlier tasks, generalization to later tasks, and the ability to keep patching. GEM supplies measurement instruments for the first two (backward and forward transfer). This paper supplies the third: intransigence is the ability to keep patching, written as a subtraction against an explicit reference, and Table 3 is the trade-off our claim asserts, demonstrated in weight space — turning the retention dial from $\lambda = 75$ to $\lambda = 75\times10^5$ buys $-0.06$ in forgetting and pays $+0.14$ in intransigence. Our named falsifier is a plasticity-blind, per-edit-gated system with a swept retention budget; Table 3 and the Harness Continual Learning tolerance sweep are the same experiment, and this paper is the older and cleaner instance of it. What the paper does not do is plot either measure against stream position, so the decline itself — the thing our claim is about — is not demonstrated here; only the trade-off at a point is.

**Building the accuracy table under score-before-update.** Our protocol scores task $t$ with harness $h_t$, the harness as it stood before task $t$ arrived, and edits afterward to $h_{t+1}$; earlier tasks can be re-run. Write $a_{k,j}$ for the score on task $j$ obtained by running it under harness $h_k$. Two families of entries exist and they are not the same thing:

- The **arrival score** $a_{t-1,t}$, produced by the stream itself, one per task, free. This is the cell GEM's forward transfer reads.
- The **re-run scores** $a_{k,j}$ for any $k$ and $j$, produced by replaying a stored task under a later harness. The anchor set already defined in `docs/framing.md` — previously passed tasks, re-checked at commit time — is exactly the mechanism that fills row $k$ of this table. This is where the cost lives: one re-run per anchor task per commit.

Note the index shift against the paper. This paper's diagonal $a_{k,k}$ is the score on task $k$ *after* training on it; our arrival score $a_{t-1,t}$ is the score *before* the edit derived from it. They sit in adjacent cells and answer different questions.

**The forgetting measure transfers unchanged.** $f_j^k = \max_{l\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}} a_{l,j} - a_{k,j}$, averaged over $j < k$, computed entirely from re-run scores. The maximum rather than the diagonal is the right choice for a harness, and more clearly right than it is for a network: a harness edit made for task 40 can raise an old task's score because it generalized — a promotion that replaced several narrow entries with one general entry is precisely such an edit — and a later edit can take that gain away again. GEM's diagonal anchor would not see either event; this paper's maximum sees both. The cost is that every row must be stored, not just the first and last.

**Intransigence needs a diagonal entry and a reference, and we must choose both.** For the diagonal, the honest option is to re-run task $k$ once under $h_k$, the harness after its own edit, giving $a_{k,k}$ exactly as the paper defines it: after being given the chance to patch for this task, how far short did the harness land. This costs one extra run per task and does not violate score-before-update, because the number reported as stream performance remains the arrival score $a_{k-1,k}$; the re-run is a second, separately labelled measurement. Using the arrival score in place of the diagonal would measure something else — mostly forward transfer, since a harness that has never met this kind of task scores low there for reasons that are not a patching failure.

For the reference $a_k^*$, three candidates, and the paper's own permission to approximate ("a reasonable reference/target model can be defined depending on the feasibility to obtain it"):

1. **A joint harness** — built from scratch with all of tasks 1 to $k$ available at once. The literal port of $\bigcup_{l\le k}\mathcal{D}_l$, and the honest ceiling. It costs a full harness rebuild per value of $k$, and on a heterogeneous stream it is not obviously the best harness for task $k$, because it also has to serve the other $k-1$ tasks. This is the faithful choice and the expensive one.
2. **A fresh harness given the same batch** — the base harness plus edits derived from task $k$ alone, one edit from $h_0$ on one batch. This is cheap, it is the same operation the stream performs at every step, and it isolates the ability to patch from every history effect: the only difference between $a_k^*$ and $a_{k,k}$ is the accumulated harness. It is also the reference the Nature loss-of-plasticity paper actually uses in its class-incremental experiment — a learner retrained from scratch on the data available now — so choosing it aligns our measure with that literature rather than with the joint-training literature.
3. **The frozen base model, or the unedited base harness $h_0$** — this is GEM's untrained-network baseline, a floor rather than a ceiling. Scoring against it gives forward transfer in GEM's sense, $a_{k-1,k} - a_{0,k}$, not intransigence. It costs almost nothing and should be recorded anyway, because it is the denominator for the forward-transfer half of our claim.

**Recommendation: option 2 as the intransigence reference, option 3 recorded alongside as the forward-transfer baseline, option 1 reserved for a single end-of-stream check.** Option 2 is what "the ability to keep patching" means — could a harness that carries no history have been patched for this task — and it is the only one of the three whose cost is constant per task rather than growing with $k$.

**Which measure best matches the ability to keep patching: intransigence, with the specialist reference, reported as a curve against stream position.** Not the single final number this paper publishes. The curve is where our claim lives: a greedy harness should show $I_k$ rising with $k$ while a plasticity-aware one does not. The paper gives the formula and never draws that curve, which is exactly the gap our work would fill, and it is cheap to fill under option 2.

**Four further things this paper hands us.**

1. *The single-head warning is a warning about our own evaluations.* The head setting is whether the system is told which task it is facing. A harness with a router or a task-type detector, or an evaluation that groups tasks by family, is the multi-head setting; a harness serving an unlabelled heterogeneous stream is the single-head setting. Table 1 shows that the multi-head setting makes forgetting (0.001) and intransigence (0.01) both vanish for every regularized method while the single-head setting puts them at 0.08 and 0.77 for the same method. Any harness evaluation that hands the system its task label will show no forgetting and no intransigence and will be measuring nothing.
2. *A tiny amount of retained material removes most of the intransigence, and that is the baseline to beat.* Ten samples per previous class, 0.2% of MNIST, took PI's intransigence from 0.80 to 0.05 and its accuracy from 57.6% to 78.7%. The harness analogue is keeping a few worked examples per past task in the memory layer. This is cheap, obvious, and strong, and any plasticity mechanism we propose has to be compared against it rather than against an unregularized baseline.
3. *Never report intransigence without forgetting.* A harness that has overfitted to the latest task family will look maximally plastic by this measure alone. Table 1's CIFAR-100 single-head rows are the cautionary case: $I_{10} = -0.06$ at 10.2% average accuracy, and $I_{10} = -0.3$ at 12.9% accuracy with forgetting 0.64. Fig. 3's quadrant plot — the two measures as one point, with the ideal corner marked — is the right figure shape to copy.
4. *The vocabulary gap is real and needs the researcher's decision.* `docs/framing.md`'s Terms table has no entry for the third future quantity; the phrase in the Claim is "the ability to keep patching". This paper supplies the field's standard name for it, together with a formula. Adding it to the allowlist is a framing amendment, not an agent's call.

**What does not transfer.** The whole RWalk mechanism: a Fisher matrix, a diagonal approximation of a KL divergence, a quadratic penalty on parameter displacement, and a path integral over an optimization trajectory all require a differentiable parameter vector, and a harness is text. There is no Fisher of a prompt. The three elaborate sample-selection rules (plane distance, entropy, mean of features) all need a learned feature space and a decision boundary; the paper's own Fig. 5 finds uniform sampling as good as all of them for regularized methods, which is the finding to carry rather than the rules. The single-head confusion mechanism — a shared softmax over a growing label set — is specific to classification; a harness fails on an unlabelled heterogeneous stream for different reasons, most likely retrieval of the wrong accumulated entry rather than a diluted output distribution. And the streams here are five, ten and twenty tasks of image classification with one loss, so nothing in the results speaks to heterogeneity, recurrence, or hundreds of edits.

## 6. Summary

### a) One-sentence core idea

Judge an incremental learner by two numbers, how much it forgot and how much it failed to learn, and regularize with importance accumulated along the whole optimization path.

### b) Quick-reference pipeline

1. Split a dataset into tasks with disjoint label sets and train on them one after another, recording after each task the accuracy on every task seen so far.
2. Report forgetting as, for each earlier task, its best score anywhere in the run minus its score now, averaged over earlier tasks.
3. Separately train an ordinary model on all the data available up to the new task, and report intransigence as that model's score on the new task minus the incremental learner's score on the new task.
4. Report both numbers in both evaluation settings — with the task identity given at test time and without it — because the first setting makes every method look finished.
5. As a method, penalize each parameter for moving away from its previous value, weighted by an online estimate of how much the network's output distribution depends on it plus how much loss it removed per unit of output change along the whole path, and average those weights at each task boundary so the penalty does not stiffen.
